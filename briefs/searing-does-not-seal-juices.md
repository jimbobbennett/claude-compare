## Key facts
- The belief that searing seals in juices traces to German chemist Justus von Liebig, who published "Researches on the Chemistry of Food" in 1847 (https://www.chowhound.com/1560060/myth-searing-steak-juices/)
- Liebig's original claim concerned boiling, not pan-searing; later reinterpreted and applied to searing (https://www.chowhound.com/1560060/myth-searing-steak-juices/)
- Auguste Escoffier and subsequent professional cookbooks propagated the claim into 20th-century culinary orthodoxy (https://www.exploratorium.edu/food/searing-steak)
- Liebig's biographer W. A. Shenstone restated the theory in 1901 as "heating as strongly as possible at first" to retard juice escape (https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/myth-searing-steaks-seals-juices/)
- Concept was debunked in the early 1900s yet persists in restaurant marketing copy and culinary reference works (https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/myth-searing-steaks-seals-juices/)
- Cook's Illustrated / America's Test Kitchen ran a controlled comparison of sear-first vs. sear-last ribeyes and found essentially identical weight loss (https://www.americastestkitchen.com/cooksillustrated/how_tos/5699-searing-steak)
- Harold McGee's mechanistic point: the crust is not waterproof — audible sizzling during cooking is moisture escaping and vaporizing (https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/myth-searing-steaks-seals-juices/)
- No Maillard reaction product forms a water barrier; Maillard creates flavor and aroma compounds, not a seal (https://www.exploratorium.edu/food/searing-steak)
- Searing at high heat can increase net moisture loss relative to cooking to the same internal temperature without a pre-sear (https://www.bbq-brethren.com/forum/showthread.php?t=22883)
- Real mechanism of juice loss is thermal protein denaturation: myofibrillar proteins denature and shrink, expelling water held in the muscle structure (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9738884/)
- Transverse muscle-fibre shrinkage occurs mostly at 40–60 °C, driven by myofibrillar protein denaturation, reducing water-holding capacity (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9738884/)
- At higher temperatures myosin and actin denature severely and collagen converts to gelatin, further increasing juice loss (https://academic.oup.com/ijfst/advance-article-pdf/doi/10.1093/ijfood/vvaf076/62828703/vvaf076.pdf)
- Reverse sear (low oven first, high-heat sear last) is the technique implication: reduces the grey overcooked band and yields wall-to-wall pink (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Searing)
- Kenji López-Alt's roast test: sear-first lost more juice than roast-first-then-sear (https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/myth-searing-steaks-seals-juices/)
- Searing has genuine non-moisture benefits: flavor, crust texture contrast, appearance, surface pathogen reduction (https://www.bbq-brethren.com/forum/showthread.php?t=22883)

## Figures
- 1847 — publication year of Liebig's "Researches on the Chemistry of Food" (https://www.chowhound.com/1560060/myth-searing-steak-juices/)
- ~150 years — span over which the myth persisted in mainstream culinary teaching (https://reluctantgourmet.com/searing-meat-and-juiciness/)
- ~22% of starting weight — liquid lost by BOTH sear-first and sear-last ribeye batches in the Cook's Illustrated test (https://www.americastestkitchen.com/cooksillustrated/how_tos/5699-searing-steak)
- 8 ribeye steaks, 1¼-inch thick, split into two batches — Cook's Illustrated sample (https://www.americastestkitchen.com/cooksillustrated/how_tos/5699-searing-steak)
- 250 °F oven, cooked to 125 °F internal — Cook's Illustrated protocol (https://www.americastestkitchen.com/cooksillustrated/how_tos/5699-searing-steak)
- 13% vs. 19% weight loss — unseared vs. seared steak in Alton Brown's 2008 test (https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/myth-searing-steaks-seals-juices/)
- 1.68% — extra juice lost by sear-then-roast vs. roast-then-sear in López-Alt's test (https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/myth-searing-steaks-seals-juices/)
- Meat is roughly 70% water (https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/myth-searing-steaks-seals-juices/)
- ~75 g water per 100 g living muscle, ~85% of it intracellular (https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4615-4731-0_21)
- Maillard browning proceeds rapidly from ~140–165 °C (280–330 °F) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maillard_reaction)
- 40–60 °C — range of transverse myofibrillar shrinkage during cooking (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9738884/)
- 500–550 °F — typical high-heat searing temperatures cited by López-Alt (https://www.bostonglobe.com/lifestyle/food-dining/2016/03/17/kenji-lopez-alt-tells-you-how-cook-meat/SrctX5RDdiLtebVObxeRiK/story.html)
- 1–1.5 in (25–38 mm) — thickness threshold at which reverse sear is recommended (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Searing)
- 1,800 °F broiler — steakhouse marketing claim still invoking "seal in the juices" (https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/myth-searing-steaks-seals-juices/)
- December 1995 — Cook's Illustrated prime rib comparison issue (https://www.bbq-brethren.com/threads/searing-myth.22883/)

## Terms to define
- Maillard reaction — non-enzymatic browning between amino acids and reducing sugars producing flavor/aroma compounds
- Caramelization — browning of sugars alone; distinct from Maillard
- Water-holding capacity (WHC) — meat's ability to retain its own water during handling and cooking
- Myofibril — contractile protein bundle inside a muscle fibre; shrinks on heating
- Myosin / actin — principal muscle proteins whose denaturation drives shrinkage and juice expulsion
- Denaturation — heat-induced unfolding of protein structure
- Drip / purge — fluid exuded from raw meat before cooking
- Cooking loss — mass lost during cooking as evaporated water plus expelled juice and fat
- Reverse sear — low-temperature cook to near-target internal temp, then high-heat sear at the end
- Grey band — overcooked greyish zone between crust and pink interior
- Sous vide — vacuum-sealed cooking in precisely temperature-controlled water bath
- Pyrolysis — thermal breakdown past browning, producing acrid burnt flavors
