## Key facts
- Elmore Leonard's 2001 New York Times list of writing rules made "Avoid prologues" rule No. 2, framing the prologue as displaceable backstory (https://fs.blog/elmore-leonard-10-rules-of-writing/)
- Standard craft/editorial advice remains anti-prologue: editors argue it front-loads backstory and creates "false tension" by giving the reader more information than the protagonist (https://www.goodstorycompany.com/blog/novel-prologue)
- Agent-side guidance is split: some advise sending Chapter One instead of the prologue in short query samples because of a widespread "prologue prejudice" among agents (https://foxprinteditorial.com/2023/02/09/should-i-include-a-prologue-in-a-query-and-other-prologue-faqs/)
- Opposing agent-side guidance says the prologue is the opening of the book and must be submitted with sample pages (https://www.deareditor.com/2024/01/submit-your-prologue-with-your-sample-pages/)
- A common workaround circulating in editorial advice is to keep the section but not label it "Prologue" (https://foxprinteditorial.com/2023/02/09/should-i-include-a-prologue-in-a-query-and-other-prologue-faqs/)
- Agent Janet Reid's stated objection is structural, not aesthetic: prologues often delay voice, protagonist and forward motion in the pages agents read first (https://jetreidliterary.blogspot.com/2019/02/the-much-maligned-prologue-with-some.html)
- Acquiring-editor testimony disputes that any industry-wide prohibition exists, attributing the folk rule to novice over-explaining (https://thewritersally.com/articles/how-to-send-the-right-sample-chapters-to-agents/)
- The prologue is functionally equivalent to the screen "cold open," a separate pre-title sequence whose significance is withheld until later (https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/how-to-write-a-cold-open-in-your-script)
- Thriller and domestic-suspense convention uses the prologue precisely as a cold open — a short high-adrenaline scene before the slower introduction of character and world (https://www.writersandartists.co.uk/advice/prologue-or-not-prologue-0)
- Cold-open structure is identified as working in thrillers and mysteries and in literary fiction that manipulates structure and time (https://hearth.sh/guides/cold-open)
- Literary fiction's absorption of genre machinery is a documented publishing shift: cultural heat and large advances moving toward long, plot-driven, screen-ready novels while quiet domestic books migrate to smaller presses (https://lithub.com/the-rise-of-the-literary-binge-read/)
- Critics have named an "option aesthetic" in contemporary fiction — episodic plots, ensemble casts, intricate world-building — as fiction written with adaptation in view (https://post45.org/2021/11/the-new-literary-television-an-introduction/)
- Donna Tartt's *The Secret History* (1992) is the canonical modern literary flash-forward prologue: it names the victim and the killers up front, converting a whodunit into a whydunit (https://lup.lub.lu.se/student-papers/record/8973170/file/8973171.pdf)
- Critic John Mullan reads Tartt's prologues as isolating episodes of violence outside the narrative flow while remaining part of the story (https://www.languageisavirus.com/donna-tartt/reviews-the-secret-history-mullan.php)
- Craft authorities concede the pure-backstory prologue is the hardest kind to justify because contemporary readers expect immediate immersion in action (https://janefriedman.com/prologues-that-work/)

## Terms to define
- Prologue — discrete front-matter narrative section preceding Chapter One, often outside the main timeline or POV
- Cold open — screen device: a scene running before titles, meaning withheld until later
- In medias res — opening in the middle of the action rather than at chronological start
- Flash-forward prologue — opening scene set after the main narrative's events, seeding outcome
- Frame narrative — outer story enclosing the main one, often supplying narrator and retrospective distance
- Whydunit — crime structure in which culprit is known early and motive is the mystery
- Option aesthetic — fiction shaped by anticipated screen adaptation: ensembles, episodes, world-building
- Domestic suspense — thriller subgenre set in homes and families, heavy user of prologue cold opens
- Sample pages — the first pages (typically 10–50) an agent reads in a query submission
- Backstory — prior events explaining the present situation; the usual charge levelled at weak prologues
